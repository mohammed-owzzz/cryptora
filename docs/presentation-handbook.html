1 · The project in one minute

What to say first

Open with the problem, not the technology. The panel needs one sentence they can repeat.

“CRYPTORA is an e-voting system that lets an institution prove an election is honest without ever revealing how any individual voted.”

Conventional e-voting forces a bad trade-off. A central database can be edited, so you are asked to trust the operator. Publishing ballots fixes that, but destroys the secret ballot. CRYPTORA separates the three questions that usually get tangled together:

QuestionMechanism
Who is allowed to vote?A Poseidon Merkle tree of voter commitments; only its root is published on the ledger.
What was submitted?A Paillier-encrypted vector, encrypted inside the voter’s browser. The server never sees a selection.
Was anything altered?An append-only chain signed by five validator identities with a 4-of-5 quorum.

Three ideas carry the whole project:

  • Zero-knowledge eligibility. The voter proves “my credential is in the published set and this is my one-time nullifier” without revealing which leaf they are.
  • Encryption, not publication. The ballot is a ciphertext. Totals are decrypted in aggregate only.
  • Evidence instead of trust. Hashes, Merkle roots and Ed25519 signatures can be recomputed by an independent auditor role.
Say this explicitly in the first minute “The consortium is simulated inside one server process; the cryptography and signatures are real. I’ll separate what is genuinely implemented from what a production deployment would add.” That sentence buys credibility and prevents the panel from “catching” you later.

The honest architecture

Client

React + TypeScript + Vite. Runs the Groth16 prover and Paillier encryption in the browser. Three workspaces: administrator, voter, auditor.

Server

Express + TypeScript. Role-scoped APIs, same-origin mutation guard, session cookies, Merkle tree construction, proof verification, ledger commits and reports.

Cryptography

Circom circuit (depth 13, Poseidon over BN254), Groth16 proofs, Paillier 1024-bit tally keys, SHA-256 commitments, Ed25519 validator signatures.

Data

SQLite through Prisma. Deterministic seed: 8 elections, 8,526 ballots, 428 blocks, 17,080 transactions, 5 validators.

2 · How a ballot actually moves

The eight-step story

This is the core explanation. Draw it on the board or point at /how-it-works while you speak.

  1. Enrolment. Each voter gets a secret credential salt. A commitment — a Poseidon hash of the salt — becomes a leaf of the election’s Merkle tree. The root is published on the ledger when the election opens.
  2. Authentication. The voter signs in with their voter number and password. The session cookie is HttpOnly and same-site; every API route checks the role on the server, not in the UI.
  3. Witness delivery. GET /api/voter/elections/:id/witness returns only that voter’s Merkle path, secret and nullifier, over the authenticated session and with no-store. It refuses if the election is not live, the window is closed, the voter is ineligible, or a ballot already exists.
  4. Proof generation. The browser runs Groth16 (snarkjs, eligibility.wasm + eligibility_final.zkey) to prove membership in the published root and correct nullifier derivation. The private inputs never leave the tab.
  5. Local verification. The proof is verified against the circuit key before anything is sent. An invalid ballot is never transmitted.
  6. Encryption. The selected candidate becomes a one-hot vector, encrypted with the election’s Paillier public key, then bound into a SHA-256 commitment.
  7. Consensus. The server re-verifies the proof against the stored root, election id and expected nullifier, checks the commitment, enforces the single-use nullifier, collects five validator signatures (quorum 4) and appends a block.
  8. Receipt. The voter receives a receipt code, block number and transaction hash. The ledger stores ciphertext, never a candidate name.
The one-line version “Eligibility is proven, the choice is encrypted, the submission is signed, and the receipt proves existence without revealing content.”

Why each piece exists

PieceWhat it preventsWhat it does not do
Merkle rootFake or ineligible voters being added silently.Does not hide that you are eligible — only which leaf you are.
Groth16 proofSubmitting a ballot without a valid credential.Does not prove the encrypted vector is exactly one-hot.
NullifierVoting twice with the same credential.Is not an identity and cannot be linked across elections.
Paillier ciphertextAnyone reading individual selections.Does not by itself prove the tally was decrypted honestly.
Validator signaturesSilent edits to history after the fact.They run in one process here — no real network partition.

Three workspaces, one source of truth

Administrator creates elections, manages candidates and rosters, publishes the eligibility root, opens/closes voting, publishes aggregates, exports CSV/PDF reports, inspects the ledger. Voter sees only their eligible elections, casts one ballot, verifies receipts, and never sees candidate totals tied to identity. Auditor is read-only: re-runs the full chain verification, re-checks ZK proofs independently, inspects ballot integrity and exports reports. Every portal reads the same aggregate statistics endpoint, so the numbers cannot drift between screens.

6 · Questions you should expect

Answer in two sentences, then stop

“Is the blockchain real?”

The hashes, Merkle roots, Ed25519 signatures and 4-of-5 quorum are real and are recomputed from genesis by the auditor role. The five validators are independent identities with real keys, but they run inside one Node process — it is a simulation of a consortium, not a deployed network.

“Can you see who I voted for?”

No API response joins a voter to a candidate. The ballot row has no candidate column; the selection exists only as Paillier ciphertext and is decrypted only in aggregate. The receipt proves the ballot exists without revealing it.

“How is double voting prevented?”

An election-scoped nullifier is derived from the credential secret. It is recorded the moment a ballot is committed and re-checked by validators; a second submission is rejected with 409 even if its proof is valid.

“Why not just use Ethereum?”

A public chain would publish participation metadata and cost gas per ballot, and it still would not provide ballot privacy. This design keeps eligibility and tallying off-chain with cryptographic evidence, and the ledger only needs to be tamper-evident. A production version could anchor the same roots on a permissioned QBFT network.

“What happens if the server is compromised?”

An attacker with database access can read ciphertexts, salts and the tally key in this prototype — so they could decrypt the tally. They cannot silently change history without breaking hash links and signatures, and every admin action is written to an audit log. Threshold tally custody and HSM-held keys are the production fix.

“Does the ZK proof prove the vote is well-formed?”

No. It proves eligibility and nullifier derivation. Ballot well-formedness — exactly one selected candidate — is enforced by vector-length and commitment checks on the server, not by a range proof. That is stated in the docs and is the main cryptographic limitation.

“What is actually novel here?”

The engineering contribution is the integration: a browser-side Groth16 prover, Paillier encryption, a Merkle eligibility root, a signed ledger and three role-scoped workspaces, with an auditor that can independently re-verify every layer. It is a coherent reference implementation, not a new primitive.

“How would this scale to a national election?”

The circuit depth fixes 8,192 leaves per election, so national scale needs a different accumulator or sharded roots; tallying would move to threshold trustees; SQLite would become a replicated database; and validators would be independent institutions. The interfaces are designed so those pieces can be replaced.

“Why SQLite / why no migrations?”

It keeps the demonstration reproducible on one machine. The schema is applied with prisma db push; production would use versioned migrations and a server database.

“What did you personally build?”

Answer honestly with your split: circuit/ledger/server/client/documentation. Name the parts you wrote and the libraries you used — the panel respects a clear boundary.

8 · What not to claim

  • Do not call it a deployed blockchain or a four-institution network.
  • Do not say the vote is “fully homomorphic” — encryption is additive Paillier; totals are decrypted once.
  • Do not say the proof hides what you voted for — encryption does that; the proof hides who you are.
  • Do not claim production security: demo credentials, single-key custody, SQLite, local ceremony.
  • Do not claim a smart contract or gas-based consensus — there are none in this build.

9 · Closing statement

End on the design principle, not the feature list:

“CRYPTORA does not ask voters to trust a database. It publishes the eligibility root, accepts only encrypted ballots with a valid proof, signs every block, and lets an independent auditor recompute the whole chain. The prototype proves the integration is practical; the limitations are the roadmap.”
